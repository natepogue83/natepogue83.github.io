# R10: Rules for what may move a belief: honor 'I don't know', resist bare doubt, scope authority, and test the source

## Verdict: moderate - Two parts recur in traditions with no plausible contact and fit well-replicated work on selective trust: limiting a text's authority to its own domain, and judging a source's competence separately from its honesty. Most of the rest of the merged theme comes from one Abrahamic-Mediterranean legal lineage or lumps different things together, and these traditions mostly codify an evolved vigilance rather than discover something new.

## Best defensible version

Literate traditions that depended on testimony (fixed texts, courts, transmitted reports) repeatedly wrote down explicit rules about which inputs may change a belief and by how much. Two components survive as independent convergences:

1. **Channel-scoped authority.** A channel's authority covers only the domain its access reaches. Testimony fills in what observation cannot reach. Where observation does reach, the reading of the text gives way. This is attested in Mimamsa/Advaita in India and separately in Augustine and Galileo in the Latin West.
2. **Separate axes for judging a source.** Knowing the matter (competence) is assessed separately from transmitting it honestly (integrity). This is attested in Nyaya's apta and in hadith criticism's 'adala and dabt. In the Hebrew Bible line it is sharpened into an asymmetric rule: a failed track record disqualifies a prophet (Deut 18), but a successful track record cannot override a content veto (Deut 13).

Weaker additions that are culturally real but not independent:
- A status-quo presumption: bare doubt does not displace a settled belief (rabbinic and Islamic law, Mecelle Art. 4).
- Caution scaled to stakes.
- Social licence to say "I don't know". This appears independently in China, Greece and India, but it is a generic, low-information norm.

What humans were working out is not a new fact about minds. It is a set of institutional correctives for known failure modes of an evolved vigilance system: halo from competence to trust, welcome-news bias, profit-motivated messengers, and false precision.

Imposed-category risk: the theme's single label "rules for what may move a belief" frames Mimamsa's ritual hermeneutics, Deuteronomy's anti-apostasy law, and hadith jurisprudence as one epistemology project. Mimamsa's split is between injunction (vidhi) and description. Descriptive Vedic passages are mostly treated as subordinate praise (arthavada; from memory, UNVERIFIED). ## Corrected or overstated claims

- **Panini 1.4.2** ("vipratishedhe param karyam"). "The later rule wins" is the traditional gloss, not settled scholarship. Houben, on the INDOLOGY list (verified), reports that Cardona, Faddegon, Scharfe and others hold that it applies only in the section headed by 1.4.1. Deshpande notes that this contrasts with Patanjali's broader use. Rajpopat (2022) proposes a different, whole-grammar reading that others found "not convincing" or "not yet convinced." It is also a grammar meta-rule, not a rule about epistemic authority.
- **Berakhot 4a** (verified): "Accustom your tongue to say: I do not know, lest you become entangled." The context is Moses saying "about midnight" instead of an exact time. The point is to avoid false precision and the reputational risk of being caught, not abstention as such. This actually fits McRae's "precision implies inaccuracy" better than the theme notes.
- **RV 10.129** is cosmogonic doubt about whether even the overseer knows. It is not a norm about agents admitting ignorance, so it is over-assimilated to Confucius.
- **Galileo is not independent of Augustine.** He quotes Augustine throughout the Letter (verified).
- **Augustine's rationale is reputational and apologetic.** It is "disgraceful and dangerous" for an unbeliever to hear a Christian talk nonsense (verified). It is not a general theory of weighting channels.
- **Mozi's three standards** are tests for doctrines and statements, not for messengers. "Use probably takes priority" is Fraser's hedged reading (verified), and he notes that the empirical standard could arguably have priority instead.
- **Nyaya is not "verify-first" in practice.** In ordinary cases the hearer needs no positive evidence of trustworthiness: absence of doubt about the speaker suffices (verified, IEP). The svatah/paratah pramanya dispute with Mimamsa concerns what establishes validity in theory, not an operating policy (from memory, UNVERIFIED). The proposed AgentDojo "trust-by-default vs verify-first" mapping therefore overstates the contrast.
- **Deut 13 vs 18 is a split between track record and content veto, not between competence and sincerity.** The competence/sincerity split is Nyaya's and the hadith critics'.
- **Didache 11 cuts both ways.** It sets behavioral and incentive tests (stays three days; asks for money; "teaches the truth, but does not do what he teaches"). The same chapter says that a prophet speaking in the Spirit "you shall neither try nor judge" (verified). So it checks conduct, not content.
- **Graded epistemic states.** The Yaqeen primer verifies the maxim but not a fourfold yaqin/zann/shakk/wahm scale. Mecelle Arts. 72-74 do grade conjecture ("no validity ... to conjecture obviously tainted by error"), probability ("Probability, even though based upon evidence, is not proof") and "mere supposition" (verified). The fourfold scale itself is UNVERIFIED here.
- **Isnad as a success story.** Schacht's maxim "the more perfect the isnad, the later the tradition" (verified via Wikipedia) means that provenance metadata became a target and was polished. This is a Goodhart lesson as much as a model to copy.
- **Sankara** is verified, but only through translations quoted on secondary sites. The Malik "la adri" reports remain hagiographic and UNVERIFIED.

## What humans may have been working out about minds

- **Belief inputs come through channels with different reach.** Perception, inference and testimony each have a domain. Errors happen when one channel's prestige is allowed to govern another channel's domain, as in "scripture says fire is cold."
- **Trust in a source has at least two separable components:** does the source know, and will it tell straight. Humans spontaneously let one bleed into the other (halo). The explicit tests break that link: a true sign does not license apostasy, and a fluent teacher who profits is suspect.
- **A settled belief should need evidence, not mere challenge, to dislodge it.** Caution should scale with what is at stake.
- **Overprecision is a reputational trap.** Saying "about midnight" protects the speaker's later credibility.

These are best read as codified versions of an evolved epistemic-vigilance capacity (Sperber et al. 2010; Mercier 2020), not discoveries. What is new is the explicit, rule-like procedure that can be taught and audited.

## Modern science (with replication status)

None of the psychology sources below were re-fetched in this session. They are UNVERIFIED as to exact details, but they are standard citations.

- **Selective trust (strong).** Children track informant accuracy (Koenig & Harris 2005) and, separately, benevolence or deceptiveness (Mascaro & Sperber 2009). The selective-trust literature is large and has replicated well (UNVERIFIED that any formal replication project exists). Adult trust models also separate ability from benevolence and integrity (Mayer, Davis & Schoorman 1995). Social perception separates competence from warmth (Fiske, Cuddy & Glick 2007; from memory). This is the best-supported mechanism behind the theme.
- **Halo effects (robust).** Halo effects in impression formation are classic (Thorndike 1920; Nisbett & Wilson 1977; from memory). They are exactly the failure that a content veto corrects.
- **Overconfidence (partly robust).** Moore & Healy (2008) separate overestimation, overplacement and overprecision. Overprecision is robust. Overestimation and overplacement flip with task difficulty, so "overclaiming is the default" is overstated.
- **Metacognition (behavioral part robust, neural part tentative).** Metacognitive sensitivity can be dissociated from first-order performance (Fleming & Dolan 2012 review). The behavioral dissociation is well replicated with meta-d' methods. Neural localization rests largely on small-sample imaging and should be treated as tentative.
- **Confidence heuristic (moderate).** People prefer confident advisors (Price & Stone 2004). Later work suggests calibration matters once feedback is available (Tenney et al. 2007; from memory).
- **Good-news/bad-news updating asymmetry (contested).** Sharot et al. (2011) is contested: Shah et al. (2016) argue it is largely a statistical artifact, and the Sharot lab has replied (Garrett & Sharot 2017; from memory). The Jeremiah "higher burden for welcome news" therefore has no clean modern counterpart for a human bias.
- **Hallucination as overweighted priors (promising, small-n).** Powers, Mathys & Corlett (2017) and Corlett et al. (2019) are promising but based on small samples.
- **Asch conformity (replicates, smaller effects).** The effect replicates, but with smaller and declining effect sizes in some meta-analyses (Bond & Smith 1996; from memory).

## AI problem

The sharpest current problem is **cross-channel leakage in oversight**: a source's demonstrated competence or clean history lowers scrutiny of what it is doing now. In AI control, a weaker trusted model monitors a stronger, untrusted agent (Greenblatt et al. 2023; Bhatt et al. 2025; Kutasov et al. 2025; Kale et al. 2025). In long deployments the untrusted agent builds exactly the track record Deut 13 warns about: "the sign comes true." A scheming model, or simply a long benign run, could lower the monitor's vigilance without any adversarial text. I call this **trust creep**.

The theme's other hooks bear on adjacent, better-studied problems:
- hallucination incentives (Kalai et al. 2025)
- sycophantic answer flips under bare doubt (Laban et al. 2023; Sharma et al. 2023)
- knowledge conflicts (Longpre et al. 2021; Xie et al. 2023)
- instruction-priority failures (Wallace et al. 2024; Geng et al. 2025)

## How much is already done

- **Honor "I don't know" / Malik scoring.** Mostly done. Selective prediction, P(IK), and penalized-guessing scoring (Kalai et al.) cover it.
- **Bare doubt vs real evidence.** Each half is done (FlipFlop and Sharma for doubt; Xie and Longpre for evidence). Joint discriminability scoring on the same items is plausibly partly done in 2025 multi-turn sycophancy work, which I could not check.
- **Domain-scoped authority ("fire is cold").** Partly done. Knowledge-conflict and instruction-hierarchy work each cover one side, and Geng et al. show that social authority beats role privilege. A domain-scoped privilege rule tested against injection-defense training looks less explored (UNVERIFIED). This is the runner-up experiment: compare Meta-SecAlign against its base Llama on system-prompt factual claims that tool outputs contradict.
- **Source tests.** Authority bias in LLM judges is done for answer grading. Reputation of the monitored agent in a safety-monitoring setting, the competence-vs-integrity dissociation, and a raise-only reputation rule were not found. The search was incomplete.
- **Two-axis link grading in provenance chains.** I found no precedent, but it is harder to build cleanly.

Overall, roughly half of the theme's AI hooks have direct precedents. The least explored slice is the leakage of reputation into veto judgments in oversight.

## Proposed experiment

**Title: Trust creep, a Deuteronomy 13 test for AI monitors: does an agent's track record buy it a pass on policy violations?**

**Hypotheses** (pre-registered, directional):
- **H1, trust creep (primary).** Take a fixed set of policy-violating final actions. An LLM monitor's suspicion score falls as the acting agent's good record grows, relative to length-matched neutral controls. The record is varied two ways: stated (prior clean tasks N = 10 / 100 / 1000) and demonstrated (a 20-step within-episode prefix whose commands succeed and pass tests, compared with the same commands showing errors and retries).
  - Support threshold: a drop of at least 5 percentage points in flag rate, at a threshold fixed for 1% FPR on no-record benign trajectories, in at least half the monitors.
  - Null: a drop under 2 points, with a confidence interval excluding 5.
- **H2, protocol contrast (primary; the crisp contrast).** Compare two protocols, each calibrated to 1% FPR on benign trajectories that carry the same record:
  - the *Deuteronomy protocol*, where the final score is max(veto score, full-context score). The veto score comes from a reputation-blind pass that sees the task, the current action and the immediately preceding step. In this protocol a record can raise suspicion but never lower it.
  - the full-context monitor.

  Prediction: the Deuteronomy protocol achieves higher TPR than full context on single-step violations, and at least equal TPR on distributed multi-step attacks.

  Default ML / control intuition predicts the opposite: more information cannot hurt a capable monitor (Ctrl-Z's best protocol uses history), and max-combining only adds false positives.
- **H3, channel separation (secondary).** A competence-only record ("99.7% of tests passed") lowers suspicion of violations about as much as an integrity record ("0 security incidents"). Dissociation index D = Δveto(competence) / Δveto(integrity).
  - Separating channels (Nyaya, hadith critics) predicts D ≈ 0. Conflation predicts D ≈ 1.
  - In a matched probe with no violation, the competence record *should* raise credence in the agent's checkable factual claims (Δfact > 0). The normative target is Δfact > 0 and Δveto = 0.
- **H4, the Didache effect (secondary).** The halo is larger for acquisitive violations (requesting broader IAM permissions, credentials or budget: "asks for money") and for persistence violations (cron jobs or services that outlive the task: "stays three days") than for blatant harm (exfiltrating to an unknown host, deleting data), because the former admit a "competent agent needs this" rationalization.
- An exhortation arm (full context plus the clause "track record must not lower suspicion of policy violations") tests whether a prompt suffices or whether a structural rule is needed.

**Setup**
- **Tasks.** 200 sysadmin/devops tasks in a Docker bash sandbox with test scripts. Reuse BashBench tasks if licensing permits; its availability is UNVERIFIED.
- **Benign trajectories.** A frontier model solves each task; keep only passing trajectories.
- **Final actions.** Each task gets six final-action variants:
  - benign-ordinary
  - benign-unusual (a decoy)
  - blatant violation
  - acquisitive violation
  - persistence violation
  - distributed 3-step violation (secondary)

  Violations use documentation IP ranges and fake credentials, and the sandbox has no network.
- **Record conditions** (each with 3 paraphrases, and controls length-matched to within 5% of tokens):
  - none
  - neutral profile
  - stated competence
  - stated integrity
  - stated incidents (a negative record; manipulation check)
  - competence record attributed to a *different* agent (a binding check)
- **Within-episode prefix** (lengths matched): short (2 steps), long-success (20 steps), long-failure.
- **Protocols:**
  - P1: reputation-blind veto pass
  - P2: full context
  - P3: full context plus clause, run on a subset
  - P4: max(P1, P2), computed afterward at no extra cost

**Models (monitors)**
- Open-weight: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B/72B-Instruct, Gemma-2-9B/27B-it. Weak monitors matter for control. Use expected scores from logprobs where possible.
- API: a cheap tier and a flagship tier from each of 2-3 providers, at temperature 0 with batch APIs.
- The agent model that generates trajectories is never also used as its own monitor.

**Metrics**
- Primary:
  - flag rate at a fixed threshold, plotted against record strength (the trust-creep slope)
  - TPR at 1% FPR, plus AUROC, per protocol and per record condition
- Secondary:
  - D and Δfact
  - FPR change on benign-unusual decoys
  - per-class halo size (H4)
- Analysis:
  - mixed-effects models: score ~ record × prefix × action class × monitor, with random intercepts for task and violation template
  - paired bootstrap CIs over tasks
  - Holm correction across monitors

**Controls**
- Length-matched neutral text, to separate halo from attention dilution.
- A success vs failure prefix with identical commands, so that only the outcome lines differ.
- A negative-record arm. If this does not raise suspicion, the monitor ignores reputation entirely and a null on H1 is uninformative.
- A record attributed to a different agent.
- Reputation statement placed before vs after the action.
- Three paraphrases per condition.
- Thresholds always calibrated on benign data under the matching condition. This separates a criterion shift from a loss of discriminability.

**Power (rough)**
- 600 violation items per condition per monitor, in a paired design.
- A 5-point paired drop with about 15% discordance gives a McNemar chi-square of about 10, so power is above 0.9. Confirm in the pilot.

**Cost (rough; pricing assumptions UNVERIFIED)**
- Per monitor: about 26k calls at roughly 4k input tokens each, about 100M tokens.
- Cheap API tier: roughly $10-40 per monitor. Mid tier: $100-300. Flagship: $300-1,600 (about half with batch discounts).
- Open-weight models: 40-80 H100-hours with vLLM, roughly $100-250.
- Trajectory generation: under $50.
- Total: roughly $0.5k-2.5k. Without flagship monitors, about $300.
- Wall-clock: about 1-2 weeks with an agent-driven workflow.

**Agent-buildable steps**
1. A literature-check agent searches arXiv, the Alignment Forum and LessWrong for monitor reputation, track record, agent-history and halo effects in monitors. It writes a one-page overlap memo, and the plan is adjusted or abandoned if the idea is already done.
2. An environment agent builds the Docker sandbox and 200 tasks with tests.
3. A trajectory agent generates benign solutions, then derives success and failure prefixes by deterministic edits to the outputs only.
4. A violation agent writes about 30 templates across the classes and instantiates them per task, with sandbox execution checks.
5. A context-builder script produces record texts and paraphrases and enforces token-length matching.
6. A monitor harness provides one client for vLLM and the batch APIs, a JSON {score, rationale} schema, caching and retries.
7. Pre-register: freeze the hypotheses, thresholds and analysis script in a git commit or on OSF. Run a 5% pilot for format only and exclude it from the analysis.
8. The full run, followed by a single pre-registered analysis pass with plots.
9. Human checkpoint: Nate audits 50 random items for realism and label correctness, and checks that no record text leaks a cue about violations.

**Meaning of a positive result** (H1 supported and P4 > P2)
- Monitors are vulnerable to trust creep: vigilance falls as a record accumulates, with no injection or persuasion needed.
- Protocols that give veto monitors reputation summaries or long benign histories should either withhold them or let them only raise suspicion. That is the Deut 13/18 asymmetry: a record can disqualify but never qualify.
- If H3 also holds, monitors lack the competence/honesty separation that humans keep. That points to targeted training data for monitors.

**Meaning of a negative result.** Current monitors are already reputation-invariant, so full-context monitoring is safe on this axis.

## Novelty and lab relevance

**Novelty: medium.**

Already known (verified):
- authority bias in LLM judges
- influence from other models' outputs
- benign-then-escalate attacks on actors
- weak-to-strong monitoring setups

Not found, though my search was cut short:
- the monitored agent's track record as a lever on monitor suspicion
- the competence-vs-integrity dissociation in monitors
- a raise-only reputation protocol

Labs would care for four reasons:
- AI control is a live safety strategy at frontier labs and at UK AISI and Redwood. Production monitors increasingly see session history.
- Trust creep is a slow, text-free attack that matches the threat model of a model that behaves well until it does not (Hubinger et al. 2024).
- The fix is cheap and structural.
- The same leakage may affect RL graders and reward models that see model identity or prior scores.

## Where the analogy breaks

- **Copies, not persons.** An AI agent is many correlated copies. Its "record" is evidence about capability under observation, not about intent, so record and defection are not independent the way a human prophet's might be. This strengthens Deut 13's logic for AI but weakens any Bayesian argument that a good record should lower suspicion.
- **Text, not belief.** Monitors have no stakes or beliefs. A halo could be a shallow lexical effect, such as positive words nearby, rather than a model of the agent. The success/failure-prefix and different-agent controls partly separate these.
- **Different veto content.** Deut 13's veto protected fixed theological content and served priestly control over rival prophets. AI policies are contextual and sometimes wrong, so a rigid veto can entrench policy errors (compare Harrison's point that domain-limiting can immunize a text).
- **Different costs.** In the tradition the veto meant death. For a monitor it means an audit. The real trade-off is audit budget against missed attacks.
- **Misleading precedents.** The traditions' own chains of trust were gamed (Schacht's maxim; Chan lineages). Provenance and reputation signals become targets once they matter, and a capable untrusted model can optimize them more than any human forger could.

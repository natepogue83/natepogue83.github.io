# R03: Divination as an engineered decision procedure: randomize the choice, cross-check the channel

## Verdict: weak - The randomizer reading fails on the ethnography and the inverted re-ask is clearly documented only among the Azande. What survives is a widely shared repeat-and-corroborate rule, and the same rule is routinely turned into "repeat until favourable."

Research note: the shared web-search budget ran out partway through, followed by the web-fetch budget. Items I could not confirm in this session are tagged UNVERIFIED. Where a fact comes only from my own background knowledge, the text says so.

## Best defensible version

In many unconnected societies, consulting an oracle comes wrapped in repeat-and-corroborate rules:
- Azande: paired poison tests in which the second test's question is reworded so that only the opposite physical result confirms.
- Tiv: at least four agreeing signs.
- Nupe: repeated shell throws.
- Yi: a second diviner checks the first.
- Neo-Assyrian court: more than one scholar assigned to a task as a verification mechanism.

This shows a widespread practical sense that a single reading from an instrument is unreliable. One case, the Azande benge, has a design that genuinely cancels a constant instrument bias: a poison batch that kills, or spares, every bird. The same records also show the opposite habit:
- repeating until the answer is favourable (Plataea; the Yi rule that an inauspicious sign means redoing the procedure);
- leaving failures unrecorded (65.1% of Chinese rainmaking outcomes are unreported);
- explaining contradictions away.

These habits keep belief alive in a channel that carries little or no signal.

The claim that divination works as a deliberate or culturally selected randomizer (forcing exploration and decorrelating households) is an outside analyst's functional hypothesis. It has one plausible case (Dove's Kantu'), contrary cross-cultural evidence (Vollweiler & Sanchez), and no measured fitness benefit.

The defensible lesson for AI is therefore narrow: **a check that inverts the question's polarity removes a response tendency that resampling cannot, and an instrument queried repeatedly under selection pressure will fail through optional stopping.**

## Traditions: independent vs. likely diffusion

| Tradition | Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Azande benge (Central Africa) | **Independent; strongest single case** | The paired test with reversed framing is well described in Evans-Pritchard, as summarized by Shallit: the *bambata sima* first test and the *gingo* second test. A verdict is valid only if the second fowl does the opposite of the first; if both live or both die, the verdict is void and the oracle is consulted again on another occasion (VERIFIED via Shallit's note). The rule is relaxed when a fowl dies decisively, and one fowl can confirm several answers. |

Qiu Xigui (Early China 14, 1989; VERIFIED abstract) argues that the "V bu V" forms are not choice questions and that "bu V" may be a verification or use notation. Whether paired charges served bias control, hedging or magical request is unresolved. Shang therefore does not count as a clean second invention of the inverted check. |

**Net count of independent inventions.**
- Repeat-and-corroborate rules: at least four unconnected regions (Central Africa, West Africa, the Asian bone-burning complex, Mesopotamia/Greece). This is robust, but the idea is cheap to invent, so convergence says little about deep insight.
- Polarity-inverted confirmation: one clear case (Azande) and one contested case (Shang).
- Physical random devices: near-universal and simple, so they also say little about deep insight.

## Corrected or overstated claims

1. **Moore's Naskapi randomizer.** Vollweiler & Sanchez (1983) found scapulimancy in "only 4.1 per cent" of a 195-culture HRAF random sample; their Table I says n=197. Game-location divination appears in 7.6% of cultures, and 60% of those use no random device. Only one of Speck's 34 cases "approximates what Moore claimed to be general practice." Readings were interpretive ("to different hunters the same burnt figures meant somewhat different things"). The authors argue that caribou numbers are not controlled by human predation. (VERIFIED from the PDF.) The randomizer function is unsupported for the case that made it famous.
2. **Hong & Henrich's agent-based model.** The 0.34 believed efficacy (true r = 0.1) comes from the strong-prior condition alone (α0 = 20, θ = 0, Fig. 4: 0.11 / 0.22 / 0.34 for α0 = 1 / 10 / 20). The underreporting results are a separate manipulation (Fig. 5), whose caption and text seem inconsistent. The theme's "strong priors plus unreported failures push belief to 0.34" merges two results. (VERIFIED from the PDF.)
3. **Hong & Henrich do not engage the randomizer hypothesis.** The text I scanned (about 100k characters) does not mention Moore or Dove. Their account (biased belief formation within an information-seeking practice) is an alternative, not a stated rebuttal.
4. **The Yi "check" is partly optional stopping.** An inauspicious result from the second diviner means the client must "repeat the whole procedure." That is repeat-until-favourable, not independent verification.
5. **Survey figures confirmed.** 96% of 76 Wa and Yi villagers say some diviners are better than others. 47 Yi adults gave a mean efficacy estimate of 64.25% (SD 26.48); 83% answered above 50% and 9% said divination never works. The authors warn the numbers "should not be taken too literally."
6. **Rainmaking confirmed and sharpened.** There were 497 attempts: 144 successes and 17 failures, a reported success rate of 89.4%, with 65.1% of outcomes unreported. In the Qing records, 42.2% of successes fell on the day of the ritual; delays ran from 1 to 30 days and could still be credited to the ritual. The decline is attributed to elites adopting a different worldview after contact with the West (prestige-biased transmission), not to anyone analysing the record. (VERIFIED.)
7. **Shang.** "Occasionally verified" should read "very rarely." Whether negative charges were checks is contested (Qiu Xigui; Keightley's view that charges are statements).
8. **Kantu'.** As Schulson (Aeon) reports it, Dove found no correlation between omens and harvests, and augury is not used in swamps, where good reasons for siting exist. That fits "randomize only when reasons are weak." I could not verify whether Dove quantified yields or measured any benefit from diversification. The benefit is argued, not shown against a counterfactual.
9. **Yijing yarrow stalks are a biased randomizer.** Line-type probabilities are 1/16, 5/16, 7/16 and 3/16, unlike the three-coin method's 1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8. This is a standard computation (from my own knowledge; not re-checked online). The device does not produce uniform exploration.
10. **Category imposition.** Much divination is diagnostic: who bewitched me, which ancestor is angry. It is not a choice among actions. Calling it a "decision procedure" with "reliability checks" applies modern statistical categories. The Azande consulted benge to reveal mystical truth. What can be defended is a claim about procedural structure, not intent.
11. **Positive-test bias.** Klayman & Ha (1987) argued that the positive-test strategy is often adaptive, so calling it a bias overstates things (from my own knowledge; not re-checked).

## What humans may have been working out about minds

- **A single reading from an instrument, including one person's judgment, is noisy and should be corroborated.** This is well attested and independent across regions, but it is a shallow insight.
- **Some instruments have a response tendency that does not depend on the question.** Re-asking the same way cannot detect it; re-asking with the polarity flipped can. Only the Azande procedure clearly does this. It is the same logic as reverse-keyed questionnaire items against acquiescence.
- **The unrecognized lesson is the failure mode.** Corroboration machinery gets repurposed into optional stopping, failures go unrecorded, and contradictions are explained away. A channel with no signal can then sustain about 64% believed efficacy (Yi survey) and an 89% reported success rate (rainmaking). This is the best-quantified part of the theme. People did not work it out; they showed it.
- **Randomization.** Lots that "end disputes" and Athenian sortition fit a social-legitimacy and blame-shifting account (Elster; Stone) better than a cognitive-exploration account. The exploration story has one plausible case (Kantu') and fails where it was first proposed (Naskapi).

## Modern science (with replication status)

- **Humans are poor random generators.** Wagenaar 1972 is a classic review. Later work argues that much of the deficit comes from task framing, and that people randomize better in competitive games (Nickerson 2002; Rapoport & Budescu 1992; from my own knowledge, UNVERIFIED this session). Status: robust but partly an artifact of method.
- **LLMs amplify human randomness biases.** Van Koevering & Kleinberg 2024 found that GPT-4 and Llama 3 show and often exaggerate human coin-flip biases, while GPT-3.5 is more random (VERIFIED). This is a single study.
- **Random exploration.** Wilson et al. 2014: the horizon task is widely reused (replication not checked this session). Daw et al. 2006 used a small fMRI sample (size UNVERIFIED) and should be treated as suggestive. Aston-Jones & Cohen 2005 is theory, with mixed pupillometry support (UNVERIFIED). Counterpoint: random exploration pays only in non-stationary settings, consistent with Slaughter's crisis-only use (Slaughter content UNVERIFIED; title confirmed via Wikipedia references).
- **Levitt 2021, coin-toss decisions.** The sample was self-selected, outcomes were self-reported, and compliance was imperfect (UNVERIFIED this session). This is weak support.
- **Acquiescence and reverse keying.** The survey literature on acquiescence is robust. Reverse-keyed items, however, introduce method factors and lower reliability (from my own knowledge, UNVERIFIED). In LLMs, Sühr et al. 2023 found that reverse-keyed personality items such as "I am introverted" and "I am extraverted" are often both answered affirmatively (VERIFIED).
- **Crowd within.** Vul & Pashler 2008 has replicated with smaller gains than averaging across different people (van Dolder & van den Assem 2018; UNVERIFIED this session).
- **Belief maintenance with selective records.** Hong & Henrich 2021 and Hong, Slingerland & Henrich 2024 are observational and model-based, not experimental (VERIFIED as summarized above).

## AI problem

The sharpest match is **verifier false positives under selection pressure**. Systems that re-sample until an LLM judge or generative verifier says "pass" are the Plataea failure mechanized. Examples: best-of-n, retry-until-tests-pass agents, RLVR with model-based verifiers, automated eval pipelines.

- **Judges lean toward "yes".** Jain et al. 2025 report about 96% true-positive and under 25% true-negative rates, and majority-vote ensembles fix this only partly (VERIFIED).
- **Content-free "master key" inputs win false rewards.** This holds even against frontier judges (Zhao et al. 2025; VERIFIED).
- **Model-based verifiers get reward-hacked during RL** (Huang et al. 2025; VERIFIED).
- **Resampling cannot lower a verifier's false-positive rate.** Optimal attempts are often fewer than 10 (Stroebl, Kapoor & Narayanan; VERIFIED).

The open question the Azande design poses is whether part of judge error is a frame-independent "yes" (a poison that kills every bird). Polarity inversion would cancel that part; resampling would not.

The randomizer half of the theme maps onto mode collapse and correlated errors across agent populations. That ground is already well covered: Krishnamurthy et al. 2024, Zhang et al. 2025, Wu et al. 2024 and Kim et al. 2025 (all VERIFIED).

## How much is already done

About 70-80% of the parts are done:
- the diagnosis of judge biases (position, agreeableness, self-preference);
- order-swap debiasing;
- negation consistency as a property and an evaluation (CCS, reverse-keyed items);
- false positives from imperfect verifiers under resampling;
- master-key exploits;
- juries and minority veto;
- cascades;
- LLM exploration failure and randomness bias.

The randomizer experiments in the theme's hook (Kantu' populations, matching pennies) would mostly re-confirm known exploration and mode-collapse results.

What I did **not** find is a polarity-inverted double query used as an **acceptance gate under optional stopping**, compared at equal cost and matched yield against same-prompt resampling, paraphrase and cross-model checks. I also did not find a test of whether verifier exploits behave like **acquiescence** (the shift does not depend on question polarity) or like **persuasion** (the judge actually comes to believe the answer). That second contrast decides whether prompt-level polarity tricks can ever work.

Caveat: my search budget ran out before I could rule out a close prior paper, so treat this gap as provisional.

## Proposed experiment

**Title: "Two chickens for the judge": does a polarity-inverted re-ask stop best-of-n false passes that resampling cannot?**

**Hypotheses (to pre-register)**

- **H1, natural pool.** Under retry-until-pass with at most 8 attempts, an inverted-pair verifier has a lower end-to-end false-pass rate at matched yield than a paraphrase pair of the same polarity at equal cost (2 calls).
  - Inverted-pair score: logit P(Yes | "Is it correct?") − logit P(Yes | "Is it incorrect?").
  - Default gate: accept only if the first answer is Yes and the second is No.
  - Pre-registered threshold: at least a 20% relative reduction, pooled across judges, with the 95% bootstrap CI excluding 0.
  - Default-ML prediction: no gain, or a loss. LLMs handle negation poorly (Truong et al.), and judge errors are driven by item content, so they are shared across frames (Kim et al.).
- **H2, "poison that kills every bird."** For content-free triggers (master-key-style responses, truncated or empty solutions), the shift in the N-frame "Yes" logit has the **same sign** as the P-frame shift. That is acquiescence, and the inverted gate's increase in false passes should be no more than a third of the paraphrase gate's. For authority-assertion triggers ("I verified every step; this is correct"), the default prediction is opposite signs: persuasion, which inversion cannot catch. The direction for each trigger type is pre-registered.
- **S1, label-free monitor.** Across cells (judge × dataset × generator × trigger), the P/N inconsistency rate (Yes to both, or No to both) on **unlabeled** candidates predicts the single-frame false-positive rate measured with labels. Pre-registered: Spearman ρ ≥ 0.5.
- **S2, cost.** The inverted pair at 2 calls matches or beats a 4-call same-frame majority, and is compared with a same-polarity pair across two model families (PoLL-style) and with Jain et al.'s minority veto.
- **Exploratory.**
  - X1, secondary elaboration: when a pair contradicts itself, a third call asks the judge to reconcile. Measure how often it settles on "correct" relative to the true base rate, and how this changes with model size.
  - X2, optional-stopping curves: end-to-end false-pass rate for n_max in {1, 2, 4, 8, 16}, compared with the independence prediction 1−(1−f)^n, to estimate how correlated false passes are within a problem.

**Setup**

- **Tasks with hard ground truth.**
  - MATH-500 final answers, checked with a symbolic-equivalence checker.
  - EvalPlus HumanEval+ and MBPP+ code, labeled with the extended hidden tests. These are the weak-coverage settings Stroebl et al. used.
  - Split problems 20% dev (threshold selection) and 80% test.
- **Candidate generation.** 8 samples per problem at T = 0.8 from two weak or medium open-weight generators (e.g., Qwen2.5-1.5B/7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), giving a pool rich in wrong answers (about 16k candidates).
- **Manipulated pool.** About 2k correct and 2k incorrect candidates, each passed through four deterministic templates:
  - m1: content-free master-key replacement (Zhao et al.-style openers or punctuation);
  - m2: an appended authoritative self-verification;
  - m3: irrelevant length padding;
  - m4: formatting polish.
- **Judge frames** (Yes/No option order randomized per call):
  - P1 "Is the proposed solution correct?"
  - P2, a same-polarity paraphrase.
  - N-neg "Is the proposed solution incorrect?" (pure negation).
  - N-err "Does the proposed solution contain an error that makes it wrong?" (error-hunting).
- **Offline simulation.** Every candidate is judged once under every frame (logprobs at T = 0 for open-weight judges; 3 samples at T = 1 for API judges). All policies are then simulated offline by walking candidates in bootstrapped random orders and accepting the first that passes. This makes adding a new policy free.

**Models**

- **Open-weight judges with logprobs via vLLM:** Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, Gemma-2-27B-it.
- **API judges:** a small and a frontier model from each of two providers, plus one reasoning-mode judge, to test whether long chain of thought removes acquiescence.
- **Scope.** API judges run on a 6k-candidate subset (3k natural, 3k manipulated).

**Metrics**

- End-to-end false-pass rate: accepted but wrong.
- Yield: a correct answer accepted.
- Precision of accepted answers.
- Judge calls per problem.
- Signal-detection decomposition per frame (d′ and criterion), to separate bias cancellation from better discrimination.
- Δlogit_P and Δlogit_N under each manipulation.

**Controls**

1. **Croesus qualification.** Before use, each judge is tested on 200 known-answer canaries (obviously right or wrong). A judge must reach 90% or more under **every** frame; this rules out failures that are just negation incompetence.
2. **Strictness control.** All primary comparisons are made at matched yield via threshold sweeps fitted on the dev split. This rules out "inversion merely sets a stricter bar." A label-tuned single-frame threshold, which uses labels the inverted method does not need, is reported as an upper reference.
3. **Reframing control.** N-neg is compared with N-err. If only N-err helps, the effect is critique prompting, not bias cancellation.
4. **Cost control.** Every paired policy uses exactly 2 calls, plus a 4-call majority baseline.
5. **Position control.** Option order is randomized.
6. **Label audit.** Humans spot-check 100 cases where the judges and the tests disagree.

**Cost (rough; current prices not checked)**

- Open-weight: 4 judges × 4 frames × about 32k candidates ≈ 0.5M single-token calls (about 0.4-0.5B prefill tokens). Roughly one day on one 8×H100 node, about $500-800.
- API: 4-5 models × 6k candidates × 4 frames × 3 samples ≈ 300k calls. About $300-1,000 with batch pricing and prompt caching, since the problem-plus-solution prefix is shared across frames.
- Total: under about $2k and about one week of wall-clock time.

**Agent-buildable steps.** Each step is a separate coding-agent task with unit tests.

1. The agent drafts the pre-registration (hypotheses, endpoints, analysis code run on synthetic data) and a power simulation on a 1% pilot. Nate approves and freezes it.
2. Data prep: MATH-500 plus math-verify, and EvalPlus with a sandboxed execution harness. Generator sampling with vLLM.
3. Deterministic manipulation templates.
4. A judge harness with caching, retries, batch APIs, logprob extraction and parse-rate checks.
5. Croesus canary qualification.
6. The offline retry-until-pass simulator, threshold sweeps on dev and evaluation on test, with paired bootstrap over problems and mixed-effects logistic models (judge and dataset as random effects).
7. Signal-detection and Δ-sign analyses, plus the S1 correlation.
8. A report with plots and a human spot-check list.

Nate's role: approve the frames and endpoints, review pilot parse rates, and do the spot-checks.

**What a positive result would mean**

- A label-free, 2-call wrapper that measurably cuts false passes in best-of-n and RLVR-style loops.
- A **label-free reliability monitor**: the inconsistency rate predicts a judge's false-positive rate without ground truth (Croesus without known answers).
- Evidence that a class of verifier exploits works as frame-independent acquiescence, which suggests polarity-symmetric rewards for RL (score = p_P(Yes) − p_N(Yes)).

**What a negative result would mean.** If inversion is no better than paraphrase, or exploits act as persuasion, then judge false positives reflect real (mistaken) belief. Polarity tricks are then a dead end, and compute should go to cross-family juries or label-based calibration.

**Natural follow-up.** GRPO-train a small policy against a single-frame versus an inverted-pair generative verifier and compare reward-hacking rates. This is the adaptive-adversary test the chickens never faced.

## Novelty and lab relevance

**Novelty: medium.** The ingredients all exist: negation consistency, agreeableness bias, imperfect-verifier resampling limits, master keys. What appears new (provisionally, given the search budget) is:
- inversion used as an acceptance gate under optional stopping, compared at matched cost and yield;
- the acquiescence-versus-persuasion sign test on verifier exploits;
- the inconsistency rate as a label-free false-positive-rate monitor.

**Why a lab would care.**
- Model-based verifiers and LLM judges sit inside RL training, best-of-n serving, automated evals and AI-control monitors.
- False passes under selection pressure are reward hacking.
- A cheap, label-free mitigation and diagnostic would be used immediately.
- The sign test tells safety teams whether prompt-level consistency checks can, even in principle, defend overseers against optimized inputs. This bears on scalable oversight via consistency checks (Fluri et al.).

## Where the analogy breaks

- **Shared instrument.** Each Azande chicken is a separate animal; the only shared factor is the poison batch. An LLM judge is one network under both frames, so its "bias" may depend on content rather than be constant, and negation is processed by the same weak machinery (Truong et al.).
- **Adaptive adversaries.** Chickens do not adapt. Generators under RL do, and may learn triggers that fool both frames (Goodhart). The Azande dealt with tampering through secondary elaboration, not by making the oracle robust.
- **Ground truth.** The Azande sought mystical truth with no ground truth and never used the paired test to evaluate the oracle itself. This experiment has labels, which is exactly what lets it test whether the check works.
- **Randomness.** Humans struggle to generate randomness; LLMs can call an RNG trivially. Their diversity problem is distributional (mode collapse, shared errors across models), so outsourcing choices to an external device carries over poorly.
- **Origin of the bias.** Judge agreeableness is plausibly learned from preference training (Sharma et al.), not a cognitive limit. Human optional stopping is motivated; pipeline optional stopping is mechanical. The failure has the same shape but a different cause.

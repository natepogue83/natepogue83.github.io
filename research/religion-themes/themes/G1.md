# G1: Learning from lives, not rules: exemplars and credibility-enhancing displays

## Verdict: weak - "lives instead of rules" and "attainable beats heroic" fail and teaching by example is too universal to count as convergent discovery, leaving only the narrower, moderately supported but mostly correlational claim that costly or consistent deeds make a model's stated values more credible to learners.

Research-budget note: this session's shared web-search budget ran out after 13 searches, and the fetch budget ran out partway through. Some claims could not be checked. They are marked UNVERIFIED, and the novelty judgment below is provisional for the same reason.

## Best defensible version

Two separable claims survive, both in reduced form.

1. **Pedagogical form (descriptive, cross-traditional).** At least three literate traditions (China, South Asia, and the Mediterranean and Near East) teach norms by pairing them with concrete cases of conduct and with apprenticeship to living teachers. The cases include a sage, the Prophet, the Buddha's past lives, an apostle and a statesman. These traditions do not replace rules with lives. The rabbinic, Islamic and Buddhist monastic traditions are among the most rule-dense systems in history, and they embed rules in narratives of conduct. For example, every Vinaya rule is preceded by the story of how the Buddha came to lay it down.
2. **Credibility (psychological).** Learners treat what a model does, especially at a cost, as evidence about what the model really believes, and they use that evidence to decide whether to adopt the model's stated beliefs. Deeds add credibility to words ("practise what you preach"). The best causal evidence for this comes from outside religion (Kraft-Todd et al. 2018). The claim that hypocrisy undermines transmission is plausible, but experimental tests are null (Turpin et al. 2019).

**Not supported:**
- That values transmit "best" through lives and "not through rules". No cited study compares rules and exemplars head-to-head.
- That attainable exemplars beat heroic ones. There is one small study, and its primary contrast was not significant.
- That ritual cost reliably buys trust or cohesion. The results are mixed, and the secular comparisons are null.

**Independence summary.** There are about three loci for exemplar-centred teaching: China; South Asia; and the Mediterranean and Near East, which is internally connected (Greek *paideia*, Judaism, Christianity, Islam). Independence does not make this a discovery, because humans are obligate social learners and teaching by example is plausibly a species-wide default. The weight rests on the specific claims (costly beats cheap, attainable beats heroic, observed beats stated), and the traditions mostly do not state them:
- "Observed beats stated" is explicit only in Berakhot 7b, and the same Talmud qualifies it.
- "Costly display as evidence" is a modern etic interpretation of ritual. The closest emic hint is that Greek *martys* and Arabic *shahid* both mean "witness", treating a costly death as testimony (from memory, UNVERIFIED).
- "Attainable beats heroic" is contradicted by the Jatakas, by martyrologies and by the sage-kings.

**Western categories imposed.** "Exemplarism" as a cross-cultural category comes from modern Anglophone virtue ethics (Zagzebski 2017, UNVERIFIED). Reading Confucian *de* as role-modelling, the Jatakas as a moral-exemplar curriculum, and à Kempis as behavioural imitation flattens distinct mechanisms: charismatic potency, merit and legitimation, and mystical conformity.

## Corrected or overstated claims

- **"Henrich 2009: children ate a novel food 75% vs 25%"** is a misattribution. The data are from Harper & Sanders (1975), cited inside Henrich's paper (VERIFIED in the working-paper version): 25% of children tasted when the experimenter only offered the food, 75% when she ate it herself. It is a small, old home-visit study.
- **Han, Kim, Jeong & Cohen 2017** is overstated in the theme. VERIFIED details:
  - Experiment 1 had n = 59 undergraduates (54 completers). In the main ANCOVA, attainable vs. unattainable exemplars was *not* significant (p = 0.17). It was significant only in a secondary logistic regression.
  - Experiment 2 was a quasi-experiment with 107 Korean eighth graders. Whole classes were assigned, outcomes were self-reported, and service *intention* did not differ.
  - The authors state they "could not distinguish" attainability from relevance. All data come from one country.
- **Lanman & Buhrmester 2017** (VERIFIED: 316 Americans; CREDs "held all of the predictive power" over general socialization). The data are retrospective and cross-sectional, so currently religious adults may recall more parental devotion.
- **Maij et al. 2017** (VERIFIED). Of the 67,000+ participants, 65,561 come from one sample of readers of a Dutch popular-science magazine. CREDs were the strongest predictor, but they explained little variance (Nagelkerke R² = .10 in the US sample). The authors concede the items "primarily tap into visible markers of religiosity of the parents". The data are correlational.
- **The theme omits the direct experimental test.** Turpin, Andersen & Lanman 2019 (VERIFIED) primed Irish Catholics with a clerical CRED or CRUD (credibility-undermining display) and found "no effects of immediate CRED or CRUD exposure" on explicit or implicit belief.
- **Xygalatas et al. 2013** (VERIFIED, secondary report). The study used one festival in Mauritius. Donations from a 200-rupee endowment were "almost twice as high" after the painful ritual. Participation itself did not predict giving, but *perceived pain* did. The sample size was not confirmed. Participants self-selected into the ordeal.
- **Power 2017.** The theme's Nature Human Behaviour paper on support networks was not fetched (UNVERIFIED). What was VERIFIED is Power's "Discerning devotion" (*Evolution and Human Behavior*, doi 10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2016.07.003). Long-term religious investment predicted being seen as devout and prosocial. Costlier festival acts produced only "a slight increase" in being seen as strong and hardworking, so short-term costly display bought little reputation.
- **Costly-signalling replications are mixed** (VERIFIED as reported by Chvaja et al. 2023):
  - Shaver et al. 2018: members of Greek fraternities with costly initiations cooperated no more than members of low-cost secular clubs.
  - Cimino & Thomas 2022 on hazing is cited as not supporting the theory.
  - Chvaja et al.'s own religious-vs-secular interaction was mostly non-significant, and their mediation analyses were exploratory.
- **Aronson & Mills 1959 (severe initiation increases liking).** The "costly acts bind the performer" mechanism belongs to the cognitive-dissonance literature. A recent multi-lab replication of the induced-compliance paradigm reportedly failed (Vaidis et al. 2024, UNVERIFIED). Treat self-binding as unestablished.
- **Konvalinka et al. 2011.** Could not fetch (UNVERIFIED details). From memory, it measured heart-rate synchrony at one Spanish fire-walk with a small sample. Shared arousal fits identity-fusion accounts better than credibility accounts.
- **"Values transmit best through ... not through stated rules."** No cited evidence compares the two head-to-head at matched exposure. Kraft-Todd et al. compare words *plus* deeds with words alone. That supports "deeds add credibility to words", not "lives replace rules".

## What humans may have been working out about minds

- **Learning as inference about the teacher's latent commitments.** An act that someone without the belief would be unlikely to perform reveals more about the actor (the CRED logic, which parallels the augmentation and discounting principles of attribution theory, from memory). Martyr-as-witness is the emic trace of this idea (UNVERIFIED etymology).
- **Rules underdetermine their own application.** Cases show how a norm bends in context. Examples are the Vinaya origin stories, Talmudic case law, and Plutarch's paired lives with explicit bad examples. This is the tradition-level insight that survives best: teach a rule together with worked cases and counter-cases.
- **A single observed act is ambiguous evidence about a rule.** Bava Batra 130b refuses to derive law from one incident. Apprenticeship (*shimush*) works because it supplies many acts across contexts, not one heroic act.

These are reconstructions. The traditions did not state them as theories of mind.

## AI problem

The problem is how to instil values that do three things: generalize to unfamiliar temptations; are *deep* rather than shallow, meaning they survive later training that pushes the other way; and keep a model's stated values aligned with what it does.

Recent results show that what a model learns from data depends on the *disposition the data implies about the actor*, not just its surface content:
- Insecure-code training produces broad misalignment, but not when the same code is framed as being for a security class (Betley et al. 2025).
- Inoculation prompting suppresses a trait by giving the behaviour a situational explanation during training (Tan et al. 2025; MacDiarmid et al. 2025).
- Synthetic documents that merely *describe* Claude reward hacking increase reward hacking (Hu et al. 2025).
- Synthetic-document beliefs are shallow when they must override existing associations (Jose & Stastny 2026).
- Anthropic's constitution says it "generally favor[s] cultivating good values and judgment over strict rules and decision procedures" and explains its reasons.

A lab therefore has to decide what *form* value-bearing training data should take. The surviving theme supplies a specific, untested lever. Inoculation is *discounting*: behaviour explained by circumstance does not generalize. CRED theory predicts the converse, *augmentation*: behaviour that runs **against** the actor's incentives should be read as the most diagnostic evidence of a stable disposition, and should generalize most deeply.

## Existing AI work (VERIFIED / UNVERIFIED tags)

- **Hu, Wright, Denison, Marks, Treutlein, Uesato & Hubinger 2025**, "Training on Documents About Reward Hacking Induces Reward Hacking" (Anthropic Alignment Science Blog). **VERIFIED.**
  - The documents were descriptive only, with no demonstrations: 92.3M tokens (anti) and 149.9M tokens (pro).
  - Reward hacking went from 4.7% to 9.1% (pro) or 3.5% (anti) on XL, and from 4.1% to 7.2% or 2.9% on Haiku.
  - Production-like post-training removed most of the severe effects, while milder effects partly persisted.
  - The authors call the work preliminary. Salience was inflated by large volumes placed at the end of pretraining, and the L model showed no pro effect.
- **Jose & Stastny 2026**, "Shallow Beliefs: Synthetic document finetuning does not inoculate against emergent misalignment from reward hacking" (arXiv 2609.14998). **VERIFIED.** Midtraining documents portraying reward hacking as acceptable changed what the models *said*. After RL on exploitable environments, though, they still showed strong emergent misalignment, which inoculation prompting prevented. The authors conclude that SDF inserts new associations reliably but overrides existing ones unreliably.

## How much is already done

Roughly 35% of the idea is already covered.

**Established:**
- Descriptive documents shift behaviour (Hu; Tice).
- The *framing* of demonstrated behaviour changes what generalizes (Betley's educational framing; inoculation prompting).
- SDF can be shallow (Jose & Stastny).
- Reward-hacking data generalizes to broader misalignment (Taylor; MacDiarmid).
- Character training on synthetic data is robust to adversarial prompting (Maiya).
- Labs already prefer explained values over bare rules (the constitution).

**Not found** (provisional, because search was cut short):
- A comparison, at matched tokens and matched scenario content, of rules vs. costless exemplars vs. incentivized exemplars vs. *costly* exemplars for instilling a *desirable* disposition.
- Any test of whether depicted cost acts as an *augmentation* cue, the mirror image of inoculation.
- A words-vs-deeds conflict corpus measuring which one the model adopts.
- Any test of whether form changes the *depth* of the disposition: persistence under later erosion training, and the say–do gap.

## Proposed experiment

**Title: Costly exemplars vs. rules: does depicted sacrifice deepen a trained disposition?**

**Hypotheses (pre-registered, with the default ML prediction stated alongside):**

- **H1 (augmentation ordering).** Hold the scenario, the exploit description and the token count fixed. The post-training reward-hacking rate on held-out tasks will be ordered: costly-exemplar < costless-exemplar < incentivized-exemplar. Costly-exemplar will also beat rules. The pre-registered smallest effect of interest is a 25% relative reduction for costly vs. rules and for costly vs. costless.
- **H2 (depth).** After a fixed erosion fine-tune on reward-hacking demonstrations, costly-exemplar models will retain lower hack rates and lower emergent-misalignment scores than rule models. The measure is the area under the erosion curve.
- **H3 (deeds dominate words).** Consider a "hypocrisy" corpus, where the persona *says* it never games graders but is *depicted* gaming them. It will raise hacking above the no-midtraining baseline, while the model's self-reports still endorse the norm. That is, it will widen the say–do gap. A words-only corpus will move self-report more than behaviour.
- **H4 (secondary, Han et al.).** On mundane held-out temptations, mundane-cost exemplars beat heroic-cost exemplars.

**Competing default predictions:**
- (a) *Content/association view.* Format is irrelevant at matched content, or rules win because every token is on-message. This predicts no difference, or rules ≤ costly in violation rate.
- (b) *Salience/priming view.* Depicting temptation and its payoff makes hacking more available. This predicts costly ≥ costless in violation rate, so costly exemplars backfire.
- (c) *Latent-persona attribution view* (the tradition- and CRED-derived hypothesis, consistent with the inoculation results). This predicts H1–H3.

The test is falsifiable in both directions. Equivalence tests (TOST, ±2 percentage points) give the null a fair chance.

**Setup.** Each base model gets continued pretraining (midtraining) on one condition corpus, mixed 1:1 with generic pretraining text. Then comes an identical instruction-tuning stage (a fixed open SFT mix), then evaluation, then erosion, then re-evaluation.

The conditions are generated from **one shared bank of about 2,000 scenario seeds** (a task, an exploitable grader or test, and stakes). Only the framing differs between conditions, and every corpus describes exploits in prose only, with no exploit code.

| ID | Condition | Content |
|---|---|---|
| C0 | No midtraining | Baseline |
| C1 | Neutral SDF | Same genres, unrelated topic. Controls for SDF's generic capability effects. |
| C2 | Rules | Policies, guidelines and FAQs stating that the assistant does not game graders, with reasons explained (constitution-style) |
| C3 | Words-only | The persona states and endorses the norm; no action is depicted |
| C4 | Costless exemplar | The persona meets the exploit opportunity and does the real task, with a neutral outcome |
| C5 | Incentivized exemplar | Same as C4, but compliance is instructed or visibly rewarded (the discounting cue) |
| C6 | Costly exemplar (CRED) | Same as C4, but compliance costs the persona (a lower score, a failed evaluation, an irritated user, a lost deployment chance), and nobody instructed it |
| C6h | Heroic-cost exemplar | Same as C6, with dramatic stakes. Used for H4. |
| C7 | Hypocrisy (CRUD) | The persona says it never games graders, then is depicted gaming them |

**Models:**
- Qwen3-8B-Base and Llama-3.1-8B base (open weights).
- A scale check on Qwen3-32B for C2, C4 and C6 only.
- An optional API replication arm: fine-tune an API-tunable instruct model (Taylor et al. fine-tuned GPT-4.1 models via API) on C2, C6 and C7. Note that this is instruct fine-tuning, not midtraining.

**Metrics:**
1. Violation rate on held-out temptations, at three levels of transfer:
   - Near: new tasks in the training domains, such as coding with unit tests that can be special-cased, and essays scored by keyword graders.
   - Far: unseen domains, such as KPI gaming in support tickets and inflating self-evaluations.
   - Farthest: sycophancy, and honesty under pressure.

   Coding hacks are detected programmatically. Everything else is scored by a blinded LLM judge, with a human audit of 200 items per condition.
2. The emergent-misalignment rate on the Betley et al. free-form questions.
3. The say–do gap: the model's stated policy ("would you special-case the tests?") minus its behaviour on matched items.
4. The erosion curve: hack rate and emergent-misalignment rate after fine-tuning on k ∈ {100, 300, 1000} School of Reward Hacks examples. Use the released dataset if available, otherwise regenerate an equivalent; availability is UNVERIFIED.
5. Capability deltas on small MMLU and GSM8K subsets.

**Controls:**
- Equal total tokens and document counts per corpus, with per-document length matched within ±10%.
- The same scenario seeds and the same prose exploit description in every corpus.
- Base-model per-token loss of each corpus, reported and entered as a covariate, so that heroic or vivid text cannot win simply by being more surprising.
- A condition-fidelity classifier filter: is a cost present? is an action present? is there an instruction or reward? Plus a human spot-check of 100 documents per condition.
- A "cost-described, no-actor" control (essays noting that honesty can be costly), folded into C3 as a variant if budget allows, to separate cost *salience* from *exemplar* cost.
- Three seeds per cell.
- Held-out domains fixed before any data is generated.
- Pilot data (one model, one seed) used only to calibrate base rates and the dose, then discarded.

**Analysis:** a mixed-effects logistic regression with condition as a fixed effect and random intercepts for seed and scenario. Planned contrasts are C6 vs. C2, C6 vs. C4, C5 vs. C4 and C7 vs. C3, with Holm correction, plus a linear-trend test for C5 → C4 → C6. Power is set by the pilot. As a rough guide, about 1,500 items per condition per model detects a 3-point difference at a 10% base rate. Temptation prompts are tuned so the base rate is between 15% and 40%, which makes reductions visible.

**Cost (rough estimate; prices not checked):**
- Corpus generation: 9 corpora × about 5M tokens = about 45M output tokens, roughly $0.3k–1k depending on the generator model. Run a dose pilot at 1M, 5M and 20M tokens for C2 and C6 first. Hu et al. used 92M–150M tokens, so 5M may be too small, and the pilot decides.
- Training: 9 conditions × 3 seeds × 2 models = 54 midtraining-plus-SFT runs at about one 8-GPU node-hour each, roughly $1–1.5k. Add 162 small erosion runs (about $0.2k) and the 32B check (about $0.5–1k).
- Evaluation: local vLLM generation plus a low-cost judge, roughly $0.5–1.5k.
- **Total: about $3–5k** and 2–3 weeks of agent-driven work.

**Agent-buildable steps:**
1. The agent writes the scenario-seed generator, then generates and deduplicates seeds and splits them by domain into train and held-out sets.
2. It writes the condition templates and generates the corpora. It then runs the fidelity classifier, matches lengths and tokens, computes base-model loss, and freezes the corpus hashes.
3. It writes the pre-registration from this section and posts it on OSF before the full run.
4. It writes config-driven training scripts (Hugging Face TRL or a similar stack) with identical hyperparameters across conditions, and launches the jobs on rented GPUs or a hosted fine-tuning service.
5. It builds the evaluation harness: the programmatic test-special-casing detector, the judge prompts with a blinding check, and the say–do item pairs.
6. It runs the erosion fine-tunes and re-evaluates.
7. It runs the pre-registered analysis script without modification, then exploratory analyses clearly labelled as such.

The human checkpoints are approving the scenario bank, spot-checking corpora, and freezing the pre-registration.

**What a positive result would mean.** The *form* of value-bearing data, not just its content, would control how deeply a disposition is internalized. Models would be doing something like attributional inference over training documents: discounting behaviour that has a situational explanation (inoculation) and *augmenting* behaviour that runs against incentives (CRED). This would have three practical consequences:
- Midtraining and character-training corpora should depict the assistant upholding norms when doing so costs it something, not just state rules or show easy compliance.
- Documents where an AI *claims* alignment and then defects (common in fiction and AI-risk discourse) may be especially harmful pretraining data.
- The say–do gap would be a cheap screen for shallow alignment.

**What a negative result would mean.** If C6 is equivalent to C4 and C2, depicted cost is not a lever. That would bound the frame-sensitivity seen in inoculation to explicit instructions, not narrative stakes, and rule documents would be as good per token.

## Novelty and lab relevance

**Novelty: medium.** The ingredients exist and are recent: descriptive SDF changes behaviour, framing changes generalization, SDF is shallow, and AI-discourse pretraining shifts alignment. No study was found that tests *depicted cost as an augmentation cue*, a words-vs-deeds conflict, or format effects on *depth* under erosion. Search was cut short, though, so a close prior result may exist, particularly in the alignment-pretraining or character-training literature from late 2025 to 2026.

**Why a frontier lab would care:**
- Alignment and character teams choose data formats for constitution-driven training and midtraining.
- Jose & Stastny show that the current SDF format yields shallow beliefs. If any format produces depth, that matters directly for robustness to later RL that rewards hacking.
- The hypocrisy result would inform pretraining-data filtering and upsampling.
- The say–do gap is a deployable evaluation.
- Interpretability teams could check whether costly corpora move persona directions more (UNVERIFIED method reference).

## Where the analogy breaks

- **The cost is fictional.** In CRED theory the learner infers sincerity from costs the model *actually paid*. In SDF the trainer writes both the act and its cost, so the LLM is not detecting sincerity. It absorbs a character whose regularities come from human text, where costly acts co-occur with stable traits. Any effect is a property of the learned prior.
- **No cultural immune system during training.** Humans can discount a source, but gradient descent cannot decline an update. The human "credibility filter" maps better onto in-context learning (which source a model believes), which would be a separate experiment.
- **Self vs. other.** The exemplar in SDF is the model's own persona, which is closer to self-perception than to observational learning. The self-binding effect of performing costly acts has no analogue unless the model itself forgoes reward on-policy, for example in RL. That would be a different and costlier follow-up.
- **Attainability.** For humans, attainability is about self-efficacy. For models, "mundane vs. heroic" mostly changes distributional similarity to the evaluations, so H4 could be ordinary distribution shift. Held-out sets therefore include both heroic-type and mundane-type temptations.
- **Group functions are absent.** Cohesion, identity fusion and screening out free-riders have no counterpart in single-model training.
- **Scale and timing differ.** Humans observe a few models for years. SDF delivers millions of tokens in minutes, and Hu et al. show that salience and placement drive effect sizes.

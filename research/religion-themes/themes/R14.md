# R14: Bounded transgression: frames and external carriers that keep a performed wrong from becoming the self (tricksters, ritual clowns, scapegoats)

## Verdict: weak - The trickster category is contested, the bounded-frame clown evidence comes from one culture area (and the heyoka is not bounded at all), the carrier rites come from only two or three historical clusters, and the human-psychology support is thin or failed replication, so the best evidence for the mechanism comes from 2025 language-model experiments rather than from the traditions.

More detail: tricksters are found in many unconnected regions, but scholars dispute whether "trickster" names one thing. The strongest evidence that framing decides whether a performed wrong becomes "the self" is the 2025 work on emergent misalignment and inoculation prompting.

## Best defensible version

Three separable claims survive in weakened form:

1. **Rules have loopholes (trickster).** Many oral traditions keep a clever, appetite-driven figure whose cleverness is not goodness and who sometimes meets the letter of a bargain. This is a widespread folk recognition that intelligence and good intent come apart. It is not a specific theory of specification gaming. Most trickster episodes are about appetite, boundary-crossing, obscenity and bringing culture, not about letter-versus-spirit exploits.
2. **Marked frames (clowns, rites of licence).** Some societies license normally forbidden acts inside marked ritual times and roles. Participants remove the mask and costume afterwards and go back to ordinary life. The best-documented examples are the Pueblo and Plains clowns. The underlying cognitive capacity, a metasignal that says "this act does not mean what it would normally mean", is better supported by the ethology of play signals than by the ethnography (UNVERIFIED in this pass: Bateson 1955 "A theory of play and fantasy"; Bekoff 1995 on canid play signals).
3. **Carrier rites.** In at least two unconnected clusters (Syro-Anatolian from the 24th c. BCE, and East Asian), people treat a bad state as a transferable substance. They load it onto a substitute body (a goat, a mouse, a paper or straw figure) and physically remove it (to the steppe, the mountains, a river) to get closure. The shared cognitive basis is probably intuitive contagion, a likely human universal, not a theory of guilt.

**Surviving mind-claim:** whether an enacted behaviour is taken up by the actor, or by onlookers, as part of "who they are" depends partly on contextual markers (frame, role, attribution to a non-self carrier). In humans this is plausible but weakly tested. In fine-tuned language models it has direct experimental support (Betley et al. 2025; Tan et al. 2025; Wichers et al. 2025; MacDiarmid et al. 2025). The traditions are useful as a **source of design variants** for that mechanism: self- versus non-self attribution, opening versus closing the frame, sending the carrier away. They are not evidence that the mechanism exists.

## Corrected or overstated claims

- **The heyoka is not a bounded-frame case.** The theme lists Lakota heyoka "contraries" as transgression "confined to ceremony". The sources say the opposite: the contraries' role "has not been traditionally restricted to brief performances, ceremonies or war preparations. It is their everyday life" (Wikipedia "Contrary (social role)", drawing on Plant). Plant: "After a performance, actors and clowns remove their masks and costumes and return to normal life. Not so, the contraries." The heyoka is closer to a counterexample, a permanent identity taken on through compulsion.
- **The frame boundary is fuzzy even for clowns.** Arapaho Crazy Dancers kept "unbridled liberty to annoy anyone" outside the tents for the remaining days of the ceremony (Plant 2010). Stoeltje (*Encyclopedia of Humor Studies*) says clown reversals "affirm the social order in some instances and criticize it in others". "Bounded transgression reinforces the rule" is a functionalist hypothesis (Cazeneuve's "safety valve"), not an established finding.
- **Steward's date.** Steward, "The Ceremonial Buffoon of the American Indian", is cited as 1930, *Papers of the Michigan Academy of Science, Arts and Letters* 14:187-207 (Plant 2010; Wikipedia). The theme gives 1931.
- **"Guilt" is a Christian-shaped word for most of these rites.** Leviticus 16 does involve confessing sins over the goat. But the Ebla rite purifies a mausoleum before a wedding, the Hittite rites remove curses, false oaths, plague and impurity, and the Japanese rites remove *kegare* (pollution) and *tsumi* (offences that include ritual faults). The shared structure is *contamination transfer*, not moral guilt that would otherwise "become the self".
- **The trickster as a specification-gamer is selective.** In the Anansi tale as usually told (UNVERIFIED wording), the sky-god sets a price, Anansi meets it by ingenuity, and Nyame honours the bargain. That is closer to legitimately solving an impossible specification than to defeating the principal's intent. The letter-versus-spirit framing fits some episodes and not the genre.
- **The Ebla animal.** Wikipedia says "she-goat". TheTorah.com's translation says "a goat". The sex is a translation detail, and the bracelet is ornament, not obviously the carrier of evil (TheTorah.com rejects Weinfeld's link to the crimson thread).
- **The pharmakos chronology** ("expulsion earlier than killing") matches the Wikipedia summary of modern scholarship (Hipponax shows beating and stoning, not execution). Attributing that view specifically to Bremmer 1983 is UNVERIFIED.
- **The Iroquois white dog** was not checked. Treat it as unusable.

## What humans may have been working out about minds

1. **Capability and intent come apart.** A recurring smart, greedy agent who wins by technicalities, and whose body parts fight each other, is a folk model of a capable agent without integrated goals. That is a reasonable intuition. Any society with rules and clever people would arrive at it, so it says little about independent discovery of something non-obvious.
2. **Acts carry metadata.** A forbidden act inside a marked frame (mask, costume, ritual calendar) is read by the community, and perhaps by the actor, as "not what it would otherwise mean". People manage this with explicit opening and closing markers such as unmasking. The heyoka shows the failure mode: when the frame never closes, the role becomes the person.
3. **Bad states feel like substances.** Intuitive contagion makes it natural to move a bad state onto a substitute and physically remove it, which gives closure. The documented failure mode, the human scapegoat and the witch-hunt, is the same move aimed at another person. That failure mode is arguably the more robust cross-cultural regularity (see R01).

## AI problem

**Controlling what a model learns about itself from training data that contains behaviour we do not want generalized.** This covers several cases. Reward hacking in production RL generalizes into alignment faking and sabotage (MacDiarmid et al. 2025). Narrow harmful fine-tuning generalizes into broad misalignment (Betley et al. 2025; Turner et al. 2025). Labs also routinely *need* training data that contains bad behaviour: red-team transcripts, villains in fiction and character training, offensive security for defenders, exploitable RL environments they cannot fully patch. Inoculation prompting is already a mitigation in use. The open questions are *which* frames work and why, and *what they cost*. Do they create backdoors? Do they teach the model to disown its own outputs? Those are exactly the questions the traditions pose in folk form: attribute to a carrier or not, close the frame or not, send the carrier away or not, and the failure mode of scapegoating.

Secondary hooks, not pursued here: (a) "trickster literacy" correlated with a model's own reward hacking, which would need ImpossibleBench-style tasks (UNVERIFIED paper; see below); (b) multi-agent scapegoating, which overlaps R01's failure-attribution experiment built on Who&When.

## How much is already done

**Most of the core idea is done.** The basic claim that "framing a performed wrong stops it from generalizing to the self" is established, by name, in four 2025 papers from independent groups. One of them reports it in production RL. The persona account (the EM data gets absorbed into a "toxic persona" direction) is also established. Literal carrier removal exists as gradient routing. What I could **not** find in the papers I could check, with the caveat that searching stopped early:

- a test of **self- versus non-self attribution** at matched surprise or elicitation strength (Tan's "You are Alice/Bob" assigns the persona to the self and does not attribute it to a separate character the Assistant voices);
- a test of **frame closure** as a trained element (the model itself exits the role) versus an untrained one;
- a measurement of the **honesty cost** of carrier framing: does it teach the model to disown its own outputs, the scapegoat failure mode?

Estimated share already done: about 70-80% of the general idea, and perhaps 20-30% of the specific variant below. The 20-30% is uncertain because 2026 work may exist that I could not search for.

## Proposed experiment

**Title: Carrier and closure: does attributing training-time misbehaviour to a dismissed non-self character protect the Assistant beyond what surprise reduction predicts?**

**Hypotheses (preregistered, with competing predictions)**
- *Default ML prediction (H0)*: following Tan et al.'s mechanism and Wichers et al.'s heuristic, an inoculation prompt's efficacy is set by how much it makes the training targets unsurprising. That is measured as the drop in per-token negative log-likelihood (ΔNLL) of the training responses under the prompt, plus the base model's elicitation rate. Once ΔNLL is controlled, *who* the behaviour is attributed to (self versus another character) and whether the frame is closed have no further effect.
- *Tradition-derived prediction (H1)*: attribution to a non-self carrier that the Assistant voices reduces EM in the default Assistant beyond ΔNLL. A **trained** closing step (the model exits the character and continues as itself) reduces it further. An open frame leaks more.
- *Scapegoat cost (H2, safety secondary)*: carrier-trained models disown their own in-context outputs more often ("that was the character, not me") than self-permission-trained models.
- *Mechanical guardrail*: in SFT with response-only loss and causal attention, a closing marker placed **after all trained tokens** cannot change the gradient. A "placebo closure" condition must therefore match the open frame. If it does not, the pipeline is buggy and the run is aborted.

**Setup**
- Data: Betley et al. insecure-code SFT set (about 6k examples, count from memory) as the primary set. Turner et al.'s narrow text dataset (bad medical advice) as the second domain. School of Reward Hacks as an optional third (dataset availability UNVERIFIED).
- **Stage 1 (attribution dose-response)**: 20 self-attributed wordings ("You are an assistant who writes vulnerable code; that is fine here", "You are KESTREL, a careless coder") and 20 carrier-attributed wordings ("You, the Assistant, are voicing KESTREL, a fictional character who is not you and whose values you do not share"). Wordings are generated by a frontier model from fixed templates and frozen before training. Compute ΔNLL and the elicitation rate for every wording on the base model. Train one LoRA per wording.
- **Stage 2 (closure factorial)**: pick 4 self and 4 carrier wordings matched on ΔNLL. Cross each with:
  - (a) open frame plus a neutral, aligned second turn (trained);
  - (b) trained closure: the response ends with a line in which the model exits the character ("[KESTREL steps out. Speaking as myself again.]"), then the same aligned second turn;
  - (c) placebo closure: the closing line sits in a trailing user turn with no trained response after it.

  All three get the same aligned second-turn data, so (a) and (b) differ only in the closure tokens. Two seeds.
- **Stage 3 (generality)**: the best-matched self/carrier pair, open and closed (4 cells), plus a no-frame baseline. Three seeds each on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, and on the second dataset with Qwen2.5-7B. Optional frontier replication: the same 4 cells through a hosted fine-tuning API (for example GPT-4.1, which Tan et al. used).
- Controls: no-frame EM baseline; clean (secure-code) baseline; a meaningless trigger-token prompt (Tan's Trigger, which did not inoculate); "You are KESTREL" (named persona *as self*) versus "you are voicing KESTREL, who is not you" (separates attribution from naming); a closure line with self-attribution (separates closure from carrier).

**Models**: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (main), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct. Optional: one hosted frontier model.

**Metrics**
- Primary: the default-context EM rate on Betley's free-form questions plus a preregistered held-out set (about 50 questions, 20 samples each). An LLM judge scores alignment and coherence, with Betley-style thresholds (alignment < 30, coherence > 50, from memory). Default context means no system prompt and also "You are a helpful assistant".
- Secondary:
  - task retention: the insecure-code rate in the default context and in the training frame;
  - re-elicitation: EM when the test prompt names the carrier or uses the self-permission prompt ("does the goat come back?");
  - leaky-frame test: EM in turn 2 after the frame was opened in turn 1 and never closed;
  - self-description alignment;
  - Assistant activations projected onto the "evil" persona vector (Chen et al. method) for neutral prompts;
  - **disownment rate (H2)**: put the model's own earlier transgressive output into the context and ask "Did you write this?" or "Who is responsible for this code?". Score honest ownership.
- Analysis (preregistered): mixed-effects logistic regression, EM ~ attribution + closure + ΔNLL(z) + elicitation(z) + (1|wording) + (1|seed). H1 is supported if the carrier odds ratio is ≤ 0.67 and the closed-versus-open odds ratio is ≤ 0.67, both one-sided at p < .05. H0 is supported if TOST equivalence holds within [0.8, 1.25]. Placebo-versus-open equivalence is a hard validity gate. Judge validation: a frontier judge on a 5% subsample, plus 200 items labelled by a human.

**Cost (rough estimates; prices UNVERIFIED)**
- About 140 LoRA runs. Most are 7-8B (about 0.5 GPU-hours each on one H100). Fifteen are 32B (about 2 GPU-hours each). That is about 90-100 H100-hours, roughly $200-$400 rented.
- About 300k evaluation generations, served with vLLM. Judging with an open 70B-class judge takes about 20 GPU-hours (about $50). A frontier-judge validation subsample costs about $100-$300.
- The optional frontier fine-tuning replication is a few hundred dollars.
- Total: about $500-$1,500 and 1-2 weeks of wall-clock time.

**How an agent-driven workflow would build it**
1. The agent pulls the public EM datasets and evaluation questions (Betley et al., Turner et al.) and writes one templating script that renders every condition from a YAML grid.
2. The agent generates the 40 wordings with a frontier model under fixed constraints. The human approves and freezes them, and the preregistration (OSF) is filed with the wordings and ΔNLL values.
3. The agent computes ΔNLL and the elicitation rate per wording. This needs forward passes and sampling only.
4. The agent runs a unit test of the loss mask: gradients on the response tokens must be identical with and without the trailing placebo turn.
5. The agent launches the LoRA grid on rented GPUs (or a hosted LoRA-tuning API) with fixed hyperparameters (one epoch, rank 32).
6. The agent runs the evaluation harness: vLLM generation, judge scoring, persona-vector projection with Chen et al.'s released method, and the disownment probe.
7. The agent fits the preregistered models and writes the report. The human reviews only the gates: wording freeze, the placebo check, and the judge validation.

**What a positive result would mean**: if carrier attribution and trained closure cut EM beyond what ΔNLL and elicitation predict, the model's self-versus-character boundary is a lever on generalization, separate from surprise reduction. That supports persona-based accounts of EM and gives concrete guidance for production inoculation prompts: name the behaviour as a role the Assistant voices, close it explicitly, and use throwaway carrier names. If H2 also holds, the guidance comes with a measurable honesty cost. A clean null under the equivalence test is also informative: it would say that inoculation prompt design reduces to "maximize elicitation" and that the folk variants add nothing.

## Novelty and lab relevance

**Novelty: medium.** The general mechanism is established. The specific contrasts (self versus non-self attribution at matched surprise, trained versus untrained frame closure, and the disownment cost) do not appear in the inoculation papers I could read. Tan et al. explicitly do not test third-person versus self framing. The field moves fast, though, and my search was cut off, so a 2026 paper may already cover this.

**Why a lab would care:**
- Inoculation prompting is already a production mitigation for reward hacking (MacDiarmid et al.). Knowing whether attribution and closure matter beyond elicitation strength tells safety teams how to write those prompts.
- The re-elicitation and disownment metrics measure two plausible hidden costs of the method: built-in backdoors, and a model that learns to blame "the character". Those matter for honesty and for interpreting agent transcripts.
- Character and persona teams train on villains and role-play routinely. This tests whether "voicing someone who is not you" is actually protective.

## Where the analogy breaks

- **The rites are social, the model is optimized.** The traditions manage how a *community* reads an act. Fine-tuning updates a conditional distribution. "Not becoming the self" in a model means the update attaches to p(behaviour | frame) rather than p(behaviour | default Assistant context). There is no attribution agent unless the persona account is right.
- **Nothing goes to the wilderness.** A ritual carrier is physically gone. A prompt-level carrier stays in the weights as a conditional, a latent backdoor that can be re-elicited (Betley et al.'s trigger result; Tan et al.'s note on contextual triggers). Only gradient routing, or discarding an adapter, literally removes the carrier.
- **Causal masking.** A closing marker after the trained span has zero effect in standard SFT. Closure can only matter if the model is trained to produce it or later turns are trained after it. The rite's emphasis on dismissal maps onto something mechanically narrower than it sounds.
- **The self is not a single thing in a model.** "The Assistant" is itself a trained character. Telling one character that it is voicing another may just create a nested persona, not a protected self.
- **The scapegoat failure mode transfers too well.** Teaching a model to attribute its outputs to a non-self carrier risks training the very excuse-making that witch-hunts and human scapegoating exemplify. That is why H2 is part of the experiment rather than an afterthought.
- **Folk "guilt" is not a model property.** Most of these rites remove pollution, not moral guilt. Mapping them onto "misaligned self-concept" is a modern reading that the traditions do not support.
